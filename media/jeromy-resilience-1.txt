Startup dependencies can form cycles even when normal operation seems healthy. If service A needs B to start and B needs A, restoring both from zero may require an independent bootstrap path. Identify such cycles in advance, document the minimal recovery set, and rehearse it. A stored backup is not proof of recoverability. Recover customer value, not just machine count — Recover customer value, not just machine count

A fleet can regain machines before customers regain useful service. Dependencies, backlog and competing work can make recovery uneven. The case emphasizes graceful degradation: as capacity falls, preserve the most valuable outcomes and relax lower-priority service where the requirements permit it. Self-monitoring can also fail with the monitored system. Keep independent signals and operational access for diagnosing an outage. Manual procedures are useful only when practiced; a recovery runbook should identify its dependencies and how it is tested.

New teaching calculation: incoming work is 100 items/second, recovered capacity is 120, and a durable backlog contains 6,000 items. Only 20 items/second remain for old work, so ideal drainage takes 300 seconds. At capacity 100 the backlog does not drain. This fluid model omits retries, variation and downstream bottlenecks; real recovery needs measurement. Define priority classes, throttle backfill, and avoid overwhelming control services during mass provisioning. Exercise capacity loss in a controlled game day and measure end-to-end customer outcomes. Do not assume a universal percentage of remaining capacity guarantees useful service. After machines return, why might service recovery still be slow? Live arrivals, retries and stored backlog compete for limited resources; some dependent services may still be unavailable. Measure useful completed operations and backlog drainage, not only node count.
