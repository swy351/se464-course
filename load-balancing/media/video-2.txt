DNS and global discovery — Discovery itself is distributed

Before a client can contact a service, it often resolves a hostname to an address. DNS distributes this discovery work across a hierarchy and caches answers near clients, avoiding a single central lookup for every request. An authoritative service can provide different addresses to different resolvers or return several candidate addresses. This example separates a client's local resolver from the authority that supplies an answer when needed. Many clients may share one cached answer, so varying DNS responses does not imply perfectly even per-request load. After discovery, the client connects to the selected address; DNS is not necessarily consulted again for every application request on that connection.

DNS and global discovery — Old DNS answers remain in use

Caching makes a DNS change gradual rather than instantaneous. Suppose a resolver stores Region East at time zero with a ninety-second TTL. At time twenty, the authority begins returning Region West to new lookups. A client asking the same resolver at time fifty can still receive East under the ordinary cache model, because that cached answer has not expired. At time ninety, the resolver can refresh and obtain West. This example puts the authoritative change above the still-valid cached copy to explain the overlap. Existing connections and client behavior add further complications, so a TTL is not a promise that every user switches regions at precisely one moment.
