Use simulation for capacity and architecture experiments — Use simulation for capacity and architecture experiments

The demo first adds capacity to a highly utilized web resource, then explores moving work into a separate cart service with asynchronous interaction. These are different changes: adding a server changes resource capacity; changing the interaction can change the user’s waiting path and where work contends. Inspect throughput, utilization, population and residence time as well as end-to-end response. A substantial response-time improvement can occur near saturation even when utilization changes more modestly. Conversely, a quick acknowledgment may leave substantial background work running.

Preserve completion semantics when comparing synchronous and asynchronous designs. Does the response mean the cart update is durable, accepted for later processing, or fully completed? A shorter response is not equivalent behavior if the user-visible contract changed. Also model retries, ordering, failures and backpressure where they matter. Distinguish uncertainty in a simulation estimate from a latency percentile. A 95% confidence interval for a mean is not p95 request latency. The recording shows simulation output; interpret the reported statistic according to its definition, and avoid transferring its example numbers to a real deployment. Moving work off the response path lowers acknowledgment time. Has the total work necessarily decreased? No. The work may continue elsewhere, with similar utilization or new contention. Compare the same completion semantics and inspect downstream capacity and failure behavior.

Make a prediction and test its limits — Make a prediction and test its limits

The interactive lab is a deliberately smaller model than the lecture’s QPN demonstration. It assumes one M/M/1 queue: Poisson arrivals, independent exponential service times, one server, an unbounded queue and steady state. With service demand S seconds and arrival rate λ per second, utilization is ρ = λS and mean response is R = S/(1−ρ), only when ρ < 1. New worked example: λ = 75 requests/second and S = 8 ms gives ρ = 0.60 and R = 20 ms. Increasing S to 10 ms gives ρ = 0.75 and R = 40 ms. A 25% increase in local demand doubles the model’s mean response. At λS ≥ 1 the model has no finite steady-state mean; reporting a negative response from the formula would be an error.

Use this calculation to explain amplification, not to claim every request follows an exponential distribution. A real model may need multiple resources, several visits, parallel branches, finite queues and distinct workload classes. Revalidate after an architectural change. A reviewable analysis records the baseline measurements, local change, mapping, model assumptions, workload, prediction and validation error. Keep calibration evidence separate from validation evidence. Recommend a measurement or experiment that would most reduce uncertainty in the decision. Why is R = S/(1−λS) invalid as a finite steady-state estimate when λS reaches one? Arrivals consume all or more of the service capacity. The assumed queue has no stable finite mean response; the algebra is not a valid continuation beyond the stability condition.
