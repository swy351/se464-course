Requirements & quality attributes
Illustrated lesson · Part 2 · Synthetic narration

Make quality measurable — Make quality measurable

A measurable requirement names the observation and the conditions under which it must hold. The search example gives a proportion and a latency threshold, but still needs a workload, dataset and measurement window. Counting registered users does not specify concurrency or request intensity. Define how failures, timeouts and warm-up periods enter a measurement so that different tests answer the same question.

Make quality measurable — Reason through the consequences

Other qualities require equal care. Accuracy needs a defined error measure; maintainability needs a relevant change task or evidence rather than a vague hope. XML, Java or a SQL database may be design constraints, but they do not by themselves establish interoperability. Distinguish the objective from a proposed mechanism and choose a test that addresses the objective. Any additional numerical targets in a teaching exercise should be marked as assumptions. Do twenty million registered users imply twenty million simultaneous requests? No. Registration, activity, concurrency and request rate measure different things and require separate workload assumptions.

Optimize the system rather than isolated parts — Optimize the system rather than isolated parts

The C1, C2 and T1 example models producers feeding a consumer on one CPU. Under the stated teaching assumptions, each stage operation has equal cost and completed throughput is limited by the smaller of combined production and consumption. A CPU budget of twelve stage operations per second must cover both producing and consuming work, so maximizing each component independently is not a valid objective.

Optimize the system rather than isolated parts — Reason through the consequences

With producer rates three and three and a consumer rate six, completed throughput is six while the total CPU budget is twelve. Giving all three components four operations per second consumes the same budget but only completes four because consumption is slower than production. This is a deliberately simplified capacity model. Real systems add scheduling, unequal costs and queueing; one hundred percent utilization leaves no headroom and should not be described as unlimited capacity. Why can rates 3,3,6 beat rates 4,4,4 with the same CPU cost? Both consume twelve stage operations per second, but throughput is min(C1+C2,T1): six in the first case and four in the second.
