Start with normalized data and a read workload

Source slides 1–5

The exercise imagines a social feed backed by users, follows and tweets tables. Publishing one tweet is initially a small write. Reading a feed requires finding followed authors and their recent posts, then assembling the result. Joins are not inherently slow, but broad access across many authors or shards can make this request expensive compared with retrieving a prepared feed.

Begin by stating workload assumptions: posts per second, active readers, follower distribution, freshness target and feed length. Indexes, caching and bounded queries matter before a wholesale storage redesign. Treat this as a hypothetical architecture exercise rather than a verified description of today’s Twitter or X. The goal is to understand where work happens and how data placement changes its cost.

Worked walkthrough

Consider a hypothetical professional discussion service with tables for members, subscriptions and posts. A member following forty authors requests the newest twenty posts. A normalized design first finds the followed authors and retrieves their recent posts, then orders and limits the result. This can work well with suitable indexes and modest traffic. The architectural question is how much data and how many storage owners the request must touch as the workload changes.

Now compare a member who follows four thousand authors spread across many partitions. The same logical query may require broader fan-out and merging, even though the returned page is still twenty posts. A small result does not imply a small amount of work. Measure feed-read frequency, author activity and partition fan-out before changing the model. Normalization makes canonical updates straightforward; it does not imply that every read is cheap. Conversely, the presence of a join alone does not prove the query is slow. The workload and access path provide the evidence.

Materialized feeds and asynchronous publication

Source slides 6–11

A materialized feed moves work from reading time to publication time. When an author posts, workers add a reference or entry to followers’ prepared feeds. A reader can then fetch a bounded structure keyed by user. This suits a workload with many repeated reads, but each post now creates work proportional to the number of destinations receiving the update.

Asynchronous fan-out shortens the publisher’s wait by separating acceptance from completion; it does not eliminate the work. Use durable publication records, resumable fan-out batches and idempotent feed entries. A million-follower author can create a large backlog, so report realistic freshness and monitor lag. Store a canonical post and appropriate identifiers so deletion, moderation and updates can propagate without treating every copied entry as independent truth.

Worked walkthrough

In the hypothetical discussion service, one author has eight hundred followers. Materializing an entry in every follower's feed turns one canonical post into as many as eight hundred derived updates. Readers benefit because their recent feed entries are already grouped by their own identifier. At ten such posts per second, this illustrative case creates up to eight thousand feed updates per second before retries or other work. Faster reads have purchased additional write amplification.

Queue the derived updates so the posting request can acknowledge durable acceptance without waiting for every destination. Then define a freshness target for readers and measure the oldest unfinished publication work. Workers can process bounded follower batches and use stable post identifiers to avoid duplicate entries after retries. Keep the canonical post distinct from feed references so that deletion or moderation has an explicit path through derived state. Asynchrony changes when the work happens and who waits; it does not remove the work or automatically make all feeds current.

Partitioning a social graph

Source slides 12–14

Partitioning by follower can keep a person’s follow list and prepared feed together. It cannot simultaneously place every followed author’s original posts beside every reader without duplication. Social relationships cross partition boundaries, so a normalized read may fetch from several owners. The graph model makes the tension visible: balancing vertices and keeping related vertices together are competing objectives.

Distinguish data ownership from replica placement. Replication protects availability and may serve reads; denormalization creates a different access-oriented representation. Neither gives unlimited scaling without coordination, network cost or hotspots. Use a small graph of classmates and popular instructors to compare partition choices, count cross-partition reads and estimate update fan-out. That evidence is more useful than assigning all relational stores one performance profile.

Worked walkthrough

Draw six members of a discussion service across three storage partitions. Give each member subscriptions to authors on both other partitions. Placing a member's subscription list beside that member helps discover followed authors locally, but it cannot also place every author's canonical posts beside every subscriber without copying them. Moving an author to satisfy one reader may make another reader's path less local. The small graph exposes the conflict before any large-scale benchmark.

Compare three different copies. A replica preserves another copy of the same owned data for a defined availability or read-serving purpose. A materialized feed stores a reader-oriented representation of posts. A cache retains reusable results under a freshness policy. All consume resources, but their ownership and update rules differ. State which operation needs each copy and how stale it may become. Hashing member identifiers can spread ownership, yet it does not eliminate popular-author hotspots, cross-partition relationships or network transfer. Scaling claims must include those remaining costs and skewed workloads.

Hybrid feeds and the celebrity exception

Source slides 15–17

A hybrid design precomputes updates for ordinary authors and merges selected high-fan-out authors’ recent posts when a reader opens the feed. This avoids sending every popular post to millions of inactive followers. The threshold is a workload decision: compare expected fan-out cost against the reads that would need an on-demand merge, including freshness and cache behavior for popular content.

The slides use a relational store for the popular-author path, but that is an illustrative choice rather than a requirement. Popular authors do not necessarily write more than their followers read; their distinguishing feature is amplification per post. A complete hybrid needs consistent ordering, pagination, duplicate suppression and deletion handling across both paths. Explain those merge rules before claiming the hybrid provides the best of every option.

Worked walkthrough

An author in a hypothetical community has one hundred thousand followers, but only five thousand of them read during a short freshness window. Eagerly updating every feed pays for one hundred thousand destinations. Fetching the author's recent posts during those active readers' requests may do much less work, especially if results can be shared through a cache. These illustrative counts motivate a hybrid rather than a rule that every post must follow the same publication path.

Precompute entries for ordinary authors and merge selected high-fan-out authors at read time. Choose the threshold using observed fan-out cost, active readership, freshness and cache behaviour, not a fixed celebrity label. The merge still needs stable ordering and pagination: a post appearing in both paths must not be shown twice, and deletion must affect both. The storage technology for the exceptional path is a separate decision. This is an architecture exercise about moving work between reads and writes, not a verified description of a current commercial social network.

Self-check

What work shifts when feeds are materialized?

Repeated read-time joins/assembly are partly replaced by publication-time fan-out and ongoing view maintenance.

Why can asynchronous fan-out still violate a five-second freshness target?

The queued workload may exceed processing capacity, especially for high-fan-out authors; acceptance is not completed propagation.

What must a hybrid feed do when the same post appears in both inputs?

Merge with stable ordering and deduplicate by a canonical post identifier, while enforcing visibility and deletion rules.

Design exercise

For a campus feed with 30,000 students and an account followed by all of them, propose a hybrid and define failure recovery.

Precompute ordinary-author entries; merge campus-wide announcements on read or cache their recent list. Use canonical IDs, stable ordering and bounded pagination; resume fan-out from durable progress and make insertions idempotent; propagate visibility changes and measure freshness.

1. The fan-out job meets a deletion

A hypothetical community feed stores canonical posts and asynchronously copies post references into followers’ timelines. A moderator deletes a post while its fan-out job is halfway through a large follower list. The job later resumes and inserts more references. Requirements say deleted text must never be rendered after an authoritative deletion check, while background cleanup may take time. Which read/write strategy best satisfies those stated constraints?

  1. Use a durable visibility tombstone checked when rendering and reject stale fan-out work
  2. Delete existing timeline copies once and allow the job to resume unchanged
  3. Change the post ID so the old copies cannot be found
  4. Switch the timeline to NoSQL and assume deleted references cannot recur

Worked answer

Best answer: A.

Record a durable canonical tombstone or visibility version, make rendering enforce authoritative visibility, and have fan-out reject stale work where possible. Background cleanup can remove old references, but it must not be the sole access control: the resumed job could insert another reference after cleanup. A timeline may temporarily contain a nonrenderable reference without violating the stated text-visibility requirement. If visibility is checked through a stale cache, its policy must meet the requirement; otherwise it cannot be called an authoritative deletion check.

Materialized views can lag. A durable visibility decision prevents delayed fan-out from resurrecting content at rendering time.

Check your work

  • Uses durable tombstone/version
  • Enforces authoritative visibility on rendering
  • Separates background cleanup from correctness

2. Active readers change the break-even point

An author has 240,000 followers but only 18,000 follower feed reads occur during the next hour. The author publishes three posts in that hour. Compare two simplified strategies: push each post reference to every follower, costing one unit per insertion; or fetch all three recent posts together during each feed read, costing eight units per read. Ignore caches and other work. Calculate the cheaper strategy and the read count where costs are equal; name two missing real-world factors.

Worked answer

Push costs 240,000 × 3 = 720,000 units. Pull costs 18,000 × 8 = 144,000 units, so pull is five times cheaper under these assumptions. Equality occurs at 720,000 / 8 = 90,000 feed reads in the hour. This threshold is not determined solely by follower count. Real factors include repeated reads benefiting from caches, latency of merging the results, active-reader distribution, write batching and freshness requirements. The cost units are a modeling abstraction and must be calibrated before selecting a production threshold.

A fan-out threshold should consider actual reads and publication rates, rather than applying the same strategy to all large accounts.

Check your work

  • Computes 720000 push and 144000 pull units
  • Computes 90000-read break-even point
  • Names relevant latency/cache/freshness limits of the model

3. New posts shift the second page

A reader fetches the first twenty items of a hypothetical hybrid feed, then requests items 21–40 using an offset. Before the second request, seven new materialized posts arrive and a popular-author source contributes two newer posts. The reader now sees duplicates and misses older items. Design a pagination contract across the two sources that avoids offset shifting, and state whether the session will include new posts that arrive after its first page.

Worked answer

Define a deterministic total order, such as publication time plus canonical post ID, and return a cursor containing the last included ordering key and a session upper bound or snapshot marker. Each source fetches candidates below the cursor within that session boundary; the merger deduplicates IDs and selects the next twenty. Newer posts belong to a refreshed feed session, not the existing pagination snapshot. This prevents simple offset shifting; truly stable results also require source retention/version semantics for late arrivals or deletions, which the contract must specify.

Cursor pagination fixes positional drift only when both sources share ordering and session semantics. An opaque offset does not supply a stable snapshot.

Check your work

  • Defines deterministic tie-broken order and cursor
  • Merges/deduplicates both sources within a boundary
  • States new-post and source-snapshot behavior
