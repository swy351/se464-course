Security: Trust, Identity and Access
Part 1 • Synthetic narration

Policies, threats and the guard

Begin with assets, permitted actions and adversary capabilities. The confidentiality, integrity and availability goals turn a vague wish for security into requirements. A guard must identify a requester and check the requested operation. The profile-query example shows why correct checks also depend on safe interpretation of input and complete coverage of access paths.

Security principles as architecture decisions

Use the design principles to make concrete choices. Give a component only the rights it needs, deny by default and keep decision logic understandable. Plan what happens when permissions change. Combine isolation and independent checks while keeping the secure workflow usable. The backup example exposes the difference between necessary access and convenient but excessive authority.

Passwords, hashes and protected transport

Imagine a laboratory booking service whose first implementation sends a password with every reservation request. Even over HTTPS, the reservation handler, diagnostic logs and downstream integrations may encounter that secret. Move password verification to a deliberate sign-in boundary, then issue a separate session credential for subsequent requests. This reduces repeated exposure of the long-lived password, although the session credential remains valuable to an attacker. Now inspect a different failure: the account database is copied. TLS cannot protect a database backup after it leaves the connection. Dedicated password hashing with a unique salt addresses this separate exposure by making offline guesses more costly. Neither mechanism substitutes for the other. Ask students to draw two attack paths, interception during transport and theft of stored verifiers, and label the protection on each path. The architectural lesson is to match a mechanism to the asset and threat it actually addresses.


Sessions, cookies and phishing resistance

A volunteer signs in to a community archive and receives an opaque session identifier. Later, an image-editing request carries that identifier in a protected cookie or authorization header. The application looks up its associated account and expiry, then separately checks whether that account may edit this image. No account name supplied by the browser should override the identity established by the session lookup. Authentication and resource authorization are distinct steps in the trace. Suppose several application workers cache successful session checks for two minutes. Disabling the account in the central store does not instantly erase those cached decisions. The design needs a stated revocation delay, invalidation mechanism or fresh check for sensitive actions. This makes the authentication service a consistency and availability dependency as well as a security component. A request log should record useful operational identifiers without retaining the bearer secret. Explain what a stolen session permits and how that access eventually ends.
