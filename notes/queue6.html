Queueing networks and architectural performance

Week 8–9 · October 28; November 2 & 4

Measure demand, utilization and response time with explicit queueing-model assumptions.

From execution cost to resource contention

A software execution model estimates the resources a scenario demands without competition from other requests. A queueing model adds that competition. A service station contains waiting work and one or more servers, where a server might represent a processor, disk or network link rather than a physical computer. Residence time includes both waiting and service; service time measures the resource work alone.

Kendall notation records assumptions about arrivals, service times, server count, capacity, population and scheduling. In the slide example, total capacity twenty includes three jobs in service and seventeen waiting. State this convention explicitly because “buffer” is ambiguous. Model choices matter: bursty arrivals, finite populations and priority scheduling can produce different behavior even when average service demand is unchanged. A useful model is precise about its boundary and assumptions.

Predict: Does a two-millisecond service demand imply a two-millisecond response at high load?

No. Waiting behind other work adds residence time even if the service demand is unchanged.

Measure throughput and apply operational laws

An execution profile records arrivals, completions and busy intervals over a measurement period. For the supplied twenty-second observation, eight completions yield throughput 0.4 jobs per second and sixteen busy seconds yield utilization 0.8. Average service demand is two seconds per completed job under the accounting assumptions. These measurements describe different quantities; utilization is a fraction, throughput is a rate and residence time is a duration.

The utilization law connects throughput and service demand, U=X×S for a single service resource. Little’s law relates average jobs inside a boundary to throughput and average residence time, N=X×R. Use consistent boundaries and a stable observation regime; do not mix system throughput with a device queue length. The NFS exercise completes 10,800 requests in 1,800 seconds, giving six per second; three active requests imply average residence of half a second.

Predict: A single encoding device is busy 40% of the time at 800 frames/s. A firmware change reduces mean service per frame to 0.3 ms. What mean service time did the measurements imply before the change, and what utilization is predicted afterward?

Before: S=U/X=.40/800=.0005 seconds, or 0.5 ms per frame. After: U=800×.0003=.24, or 24%, assuming the throughput and device boundary stay the same. Lower utilization alone does not establish an end-to-end latency target.

Understand single and multiple server queues

The M/M/1 model assumes Poisson arrivals, independent exponentially distributed service times and one server with an unbounded waiting room. In steady state it requires arrival rate λ below service rate μ. Its utilization is ρ=λ/μ, mean residence time is 1/(μ−λ), and mean jobs is ρ/(1−ρ). These expressions describe long-run averages under the model, not deterministic limits on individual requests.

Birth–death balance explains the geometric state distribution. At an empty system, departure rate is zero; at nonempty states it is μ. Multiple servers change departure capacity to min(n,m)μ and require a different waiting calculation such as Erlang C. Finite buffers change the model again: an infinite-queue tail probability is not automatically the exact blocking probability of a bounded system. Always define whether capacity includes the job being served.

Predict: What happens to M/M/1 residence time as λ approaches μ from below?

It grows without bound in the model; the stable formula cannot be used at or above capacity.

Build an open queueing network

An open network receives external requests, routes them among resources and eventually releases them. A request may visit a device repeatedly. If external arrival rate is λ and average visits are V, device throughput is λV. Multiplying by service time per visit gives utilization. Total demand D=VS separates how often a resource is used from the cost of each use.

Under the simplified single-server station assumptions, each device contributes D/(1−λD) to request response time. Sum these contributions using consistent per-request accounting, and calculate average system population as λR. The ATM example exposes nonlinear scaling: doubling arrival rate increases CPU utilization from 0.468 to 0.936 when CPU visits are twenty-six. Corrected response rises from about 0.447 seconds to 2.699 seconds, rather than simply doubling. The source arithmetic needs repair before reuse.

Predict: At two transactions/s, 4.5 disk visits per transaction and 20 ms per visit, what are disk throughput and utilization?

Disk throughput is 9 I/O/s; utilization is 9×0.02=0.18, or 18%.

Closed populations and model construction

A closed model keeps a fixed population circulating through the modeled workload. Interactive users alternate between thinking and requesting service; their next request depends on completing the previous cycle. An open model instead specifies arrivals from outside. Mixed models can contain both classes. Choosing the workload model is a behavioral decision, not a cosmetic change in how the same diagram is drawn.

To derive a queueing model from execution results, identify resource stations, connect their routes, choose the workload population or arrival rate and estimate service demand. Preserve whether quantities are per visit or per request. Check sensitivity to uncertain inputs and report average-model limitations. In the authorization example, CPU demand 0.0311 seconds and utilization 0.0933 give a CPU response contribution near 0.0343 seconds, exposing the source’s factor-of-ten error before computing the system total.

Predict: Does a fixed group of users who wait for each response generate a constant external arrival rate independently of delay?

No. Their request rate changes with response and think times; a closed interactive model captures that coupling.

Self-check

A team applies the steady-state M/M/1 response formula at 60 jobs/s with mean service 20 ms. What must you flag before accepting its answer?

Utilization is 60×.020=1.2. The infinite-buffer M/M/1 station has no finite steady-state mean at this load; a negative value from S/(1−λS) is not a valid response time. Reduce admitted load or increase effective service capacity.

Why cannot device throughputs simply be added to obtain external throughput?

Each request may visit multiple devices or repeat visits; adding device completions double-counts request work.

A stable processing stage completes 120 jobs/s with mean in-system time 80 ms. Its dashboard labels 9.6 as the mean number waiting. What is wrong with that label?

Little’s law gives 120×.080=9.6 jobs inside the whole measured stage, including jobs receiving service. It is not the waiting-only population. Use waiting-only time for a waiting-queue population or subtract the measured mean number in service.

For a stable M/M/1 stage with 40 jobs/s and mean service 15 ms, a teammate claims mean residence is also 15 ms. Calculate the missing waiting contribution.

Utilization is .6 and residence is .015/(1−.6)=.0375 s=37.5 ms. Mean waiting is 37.5−15=22.5 ms. Service time alone excludes queueing delay.

Twelve interactive users each wait two seconds for a response, then think for four seconds before submitting again. With one outstanding request per user and a stable cycle, what throughput is implied?

X=N/(R+Z)=12/(2+4)=2 requests/s. Only XR=4 users on average are inside the request-processing system; the other eight are thinking. Do not use all twelve as the in-system population with response time alone.

A storage device is busy 60% of the time while completing 150 operations/s. A proposed index makes each request use three such operations instead of two. At the same request rate, what utilization do you predict if per-operation service cost is unchanged?

The current request rate is 150/2=75 requests/s, and mean device service per operation is .60/150=.004 s. Three visits give 225 operations/s and utilization 225×.004=.90. The extra index may speed reads elsewhere while pushing this device close to saturation.

Questions-only worksheet · Worked answer sheet

1. Choose an intervention that meets the latency goal

A single-server M/M/1 image-checking stage receives 28 requests/s and has mean service time 25 ms. Its mean residence target is at most 60 ms. Option A reduces service time to 20 ms at the same arrival rate. Option B keeps 25 ms service but admits only 20 requests/s, deferring the rest outside this measured stage. Which conclusion is correct? Evaluate residence inside the stage only; do not claim deferred users meet the end-to-end target.

  1. Only A meets the stage target.
  2. Only B meets the stage target.
  3. Both meet the stage target, but B leaves deferred time outside the result.
  4. Neither meets the stage target because utilization must be zero.

Worked answer

Best answer: C.

The baseline has utilization 28×.025=.7 and residence .025/.3≈83.33 ms, so it misses the target. A gives utilization .56 and R=.020/.44≈45.45 ms. B gives utilization .5 and R=.025/.5=50 ms. Both meet the stage-local target, but B defers eight arrivals per second outside the boundary. Its apparent success therefore does not establish a sixty-millisecond user experience. A fair decision compares the full path, deferral policy and implementation cost, not just the displayed stage metric.

Changing a measurement boundary can hide waiting; a valid local improvement is not automatically an end-to-end improvement.

Check your work

  • Computes both stable residence times
  • Recognizes waiting moved outside the boundary

2. Detect incompatible dashboard boundaries

During a stable 120-second interval, a conversion system completes 2,400 requests and has an average of four requests inside its measured boundary, including service. A dashboard reports mean response of 80 ms for that same interval. Use Little’s law to test whether all three numbers can describe the same boundary. Then describe two concrete instrumentation mistakes that could reconcile the discrepancy without concluding that Little’s law is false.

Worked answer

Throughput is 2400/120=20 requests/s, so four average in-system requests imply residence R=N/X=4/20=.2 seconds, or 200 ms. A reported 80 ms would instead imply N=20×.08=1.6. Under the stated stable, consistent accounting, the three numbers conflict. The duration timer might start after queue waiting, while population includes queued work; alternatively the completion counter might include a different request class or boundary. Check population sampling, timer endpoints, class filters and interval alignment before using these metrics for capacity decisions.

Operational-law consistency checks can reveal instrumentation mismatches before a team acts on misleading performance data.

Check your work

  • Computes 200 ms and the inconsistency
  • Proposes two specific measurement mismatches
  • Maintains consistent boundary assumptions

3. Solve backward from a response objective

A single M/M/1 validation station must sustain 60 requests/s with mean residence at most 40 ms. Its current mean service time is 15 ms. Find the largest allowable mean service time under the model, decide whether the current implementation passes, and calculate the minimum percentage reduction in mean service time needed. Assume stationary Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server and no other latency in this objective.

Worked answer

Stability requires S<1/60 seconds. The response condition gives S≤.040(1−60S), hence 3.4S≤.040 and S≤.0117647 seconds, or about 11.765 ms. Current utilization is .9 and residence is .015/.1=.15 seconds, so the current implementation fails. Reducing mean service from 15 to 11.7647 ms requires at least (15−11.7647)/15≈21.57% under this ideal model. A real design should leave margin for workload uncertainty and verify the model assumptions; equality at the computed threshold provides no objective margin.

Latency constraints near saturation can demand substantially more optimization than a raw service-time comparison suggests.

  • Checks current response and required reduction

4. Choose a workload model for self-throttling clients

A laboratory has exactly 24 analysis terminals. Each operator submits one job, waits for its result, spends an average of eight seconds examining it, and then submits the next job. After a server slowdown, the observed submission rate falls even though every operator continues this routine. Which model best captures this coupling, and what should remain fixed when evaluating a proposed server upgrade?

  1. An open model with the pre-slowdown arrival rate fixed forever.
  2. A closed model with 24 users and eight-second mean think time.
  3. An infinite-server model because every terminal is independent.
  4. A model that removes think time and treats all operators as constantly requesting.

Worked answer

Best answer: B.

A closed interactive workload with twenty-four users and eight-second mean think time captures the routine. Population and user behavior stay fixed while response and throughput change. An open model with a fixed external arrival rate would miss the feedback from slow responses to fewer new submissions. For a stable one-outstanding-request cycle, the interactive relationship is X=N/(R+Z). It explains why an upgrade can increase throughput even without adding users, although solving device contention still requires service-demand and scheduling assumptions.

Workload structure determines what is an input and what is an outcome in a performance comparison.

Check your work

  • Selects a closed population model
  • Keeps population and think time fixed

5. Evaluate a bounded waiting room

An upload worker has one active service slot and room for four additional waiting jobs. In a stable ten-minute observation, 3,000 requests reach its admission boundary, 600 are rejected immediately, and admitted requests have mean residence 0.5 seconds. Assume no admitted work is lost and boundary effects are negligible. Calculate accepted throughput, rejection fraction and average jobs inside the worker boundary. Explain why using the offered arrival rate in Little’s law would miscount this population.

Worked answer

Accepted requests are 3000−600=2400 over 600 seconds, so accepted throughput is 4 requests/s. The rejection fraction is 600/3000=20%. Little’s law for admitted work gives N=X×R=4×.5=2 jobs on average, including the one potentially in service. Using the offered rate 3000/600=5/s would give 2.5 and incorrectly include requests that never entered this boundary. These observed values do not require M/M/1 assumptions, and a mean population below capacity does not imply that full-capacity rejection never occurred.

Finite admission changes the relevant flow rate; average occupancy and rejection probability measure different aspects of load.

  • Uses admitted throughput and computes 20% rejection
  • Computes average population two
  • Explains boundary and capacity conventions

6. Compare two service-demand optimizations

A two-station open model receives 30 jobs/s. Every job visits CPU once for mean 12 ms and storage four times for mean 4 ms per visit. Use independent M/M/1 station approximations, with per-request response contribution D/(1−λD). Option C halves CPU demand; option S removes two storage visits without changing per-visit cost. Compute baseline response and each option’s response. Recommend the better option for mean latency under this model, and name a condition that could change the recommendation.

Worked answer

Baseline demands are .012 and 4×.004=.016 seconds/job. Response is .012/.64+.016/.52=.0495192 seconds, or 49.52 ms. C changes CPU demand to .006: .006/.82+.016/.52≈38.09 ms. S changes storage demand to .008: .012/.64+.008/.76≈29.28 ms. S produces the lower modeled mean latency, reducing the more heavily utilized demand. The recommendation may change if removing visits violates correctness, changes cache-hit behavior or adds substantial CPU work. The station approximation and fixed workload must also be validated.

Optimizing the dominant resource can yield greater end-to-end benefit than an equally impressive percentage improvement elsewhere.
