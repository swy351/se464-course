Security: Trust, Identity and Access
Part 1 • Synthetic narration

Policies, threats and the guard

Begin with assets, permitted actions and adversary capabilities. The confidentiality, integrity and availability goals turn a vague wish for security into requirements. A guard must identify a requester and check the requested operation. The profile-query example shows why correct checks also depend on safe interpretation of input and complete coverage of access paths.

Security principles as architecture decisions

Use the design principles to make concrete choices. Give a component only the rights it needs, deny by default and keep decision logic understandable. Plan what happens when permissions change. Combine isolation and independent checks while keeping the secure workflow usable. The backup example exposes the difference between necessary access and convenient but excessive authority.

Passwords, hashes and protected transport

Distinguish authentication from permission and transport protection from endpoint security. A login verifier should use a dedicated salted password-hashing scheme, not plaintext or a fast digest. Salt prevents reuse of precomputed tables, while the hashing cost slows guesses. Moving from repeated passwords to sessions reduces how widely the account secret must travel through the application.

Sessions, cookies and phishing resistance

A session reduces repeated password handling but becomes a credential of its own. Follow issuance, use, expiry and revocation. Cookies can transport sessions, while structured tokens need standard integrity protection. Examine central validation and cached decisions as architectural tradeoffs. Finally, challenge-response and second factors need careful protocol design; merely avoiding a plaintext password does not guarantee phishing resistance.
